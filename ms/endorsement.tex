\begin{abstract}
\noindent \citet{blair2013poverty} conclude from endorsement experiments that Pakistanis hold militant groups in ``low regard.'' I reanalyze their survey with the measurement model developed for endorsement experiments \citep{bullock2011statistical}. In the unweighted analysis sample, the model implies a \PrimaryShare{} probability of a positive latent reaction to an endorsement, averaged across four militant groups (95\% credible interval \PrimaryInterval{}). Small negative mean reactions coexist with substantial dispersion. More generally, an average treatment effect does not identify the proportion holding favorable attitudes. Alternative behavioral assumptions yield different implied supporter proportions. The observed response marginals bound the fraction of responses increased by endorsement between \LowerBound{} and \UpperBound{}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Do Pakistanis hold militant groups in low regard? \citet{blair2013poverty} use endorsement experiments to measure affect toward four militant groups. They find small negative average effects on stated policy support and interpret them as weakly negative average affect. That interpretation leaves a different question open: how widely are favorable or unfavorable attitudes held?

Using the measurement model of \citet{bullock2011statistical} in the unweighted analysis sample, I estimate a \PrimaryShare{} probability of a positive latent reaction to an endorsement, averaged equally across the four groups (95\% credible interval \PrimaryInterval{}). The mean reactions have magnitudes no greater than \MaxMeanReaction{} ideal-point standard deviations, with larger estimated dispersion.

The distinction between an average and a prevalence is general. An average treatment effect (ATE) combines the frequency and size of positive and negative effects. Its sign alone cannot separate them. Under behavioral assumptions linking favorable attitudes to positive effects, the same ATE can imply different supporter proportions. Some such assumptions are contradicted by the observed response distribution. Others produce implications for quantities that should not be equated: an unaffected fraction, a probability of positive latent affect, and a fraction of observed responses increased by an endorsement.

\section{Endorsement Experiments}
\label{sec:background}

Endorsement experiments randomize whether a policy question includes an endorsement by an actor of interest. Respondents in the control condition evaluate the policy alone; those in the treatment condition evaluate the policy with the endorsement. With valid assignment and observation, the difference identifies an effect of the cue on stated policy support.

Interpreting that effect as affect toward the endorser requires more. Respondents must understand the endorsement, and its effect must operate through their attitudes toward the group rather than through other information the cue conveys. Even then, the size of a response change can depend on baseline policy preferences and the response scale. The design has been used to study militant groups in Pakistan and combatants in Afghanistan \citep{bullock2011statistical, blair2013poverty, lyall2013explaining, blair2014comparing}.

A negative ATE can reflect many small negative reactions, a few large ones, or positive and negative reactions that nearly cancel. It therefore does not identify the proportion with either sign of effect. Nor does the sign of an effect, without a measurement assumption, classify a respondent as a supporter or opponent.

\section{The Experiment and Data}
\label{sec:data}

% Survey date and original sample sizes: Blair et al. (2013), survey design and Table 1.
\citet{blair2013poverty} surveyed 6,000 adults in Pakistan's four main provinces in 2009. Their experiment covered Kashmiri tanzeems, the Afghan Taliban, al-Qa'ida, and sectarian tanzeems. Respondents evaluated polio vaccination, Frontier Crimes Regulation reform, the Durand Line, and curriculum standardization on a five-point intensity scale. I rescale responses to $[0,1]$; a mean on this scale is not a percentage of respondents supporting a policy.

I use the analysis file supplied with the \texttt{endorse} R package \citep{shiraito2022endorse}. It contains \SampleSize{} respondents with one observed answer per policy, yielding \ControlCount{} control responses and \TreatmentCount{} endorsed responses. The pooled control mean is \BaselineMean{}, the treatment mean is \TreatmentMean{}, and their difference is $\PooledATE{}$ (respondent-clustered SE \PooledSE{}). These are response-weighted means across the available policy items and endorsers.

% Original weighting and estimates: Blair et al. (2013), survey design, footnote 31, Table 1.
The analysis is unweighted. Blair et al. use population and policy weights; their survey oversampled Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Their unconditional Table 1 has 5,358 respondents and a control mean of 0.796. Differences from the present estimates can reflect sample selection and policy weighting as well as population weighting. The model-based probabilities below therefore describe the fitted analysis sample, not nationally representative prevalence.

The package sample also retains assignment patterns excluded by the original replication code: \MixedCount{} respondents have control and endorsed items across policies, and \RepeatedCount{} receive the same endorser on multiple policies. Excluding these \AnomalyCount{} respondents changes the pooled ATE from $\PooledATEPrecise{}$ to $\CleanATE{}$. This diagnostic leaves the sign and rounded magnitude intact. The Bayesian models retain the package sample; their sensitivity to these exclusions has not been estimated.

\section{Model-Based Positive Latent Reactions}
\label{sec:model}

The measurement model of \citet{bullock2011statistical} relates ordinal policy responses to latent policy preferences and endorsement reactions. Respondent $i$ has a latent position $x_i$. For question $j$ endorsed by group $k$, the model adds a reaction $s_{ijk}$ and maps the resulting position to an observed category through question-specific loadings and cutpoints. In the baseline specification,
\begin{align}
s_{ijk} &\sim \mathcal{N}(\lambda_k,\omega_k^2), &
P(s_{ijk}>0) &= \Phi(\lambda_k/\omega_k).
\label{eq:latent_probability}
\end{align}
The mean and variance of the reaction distribution are common across policies. The reaction itself is question-specific; the specification does not assign each respondent a single fixed supporter status across all questions. The positive probability is invariant to the units of the latent scale.

A positive latent reaction need not move a response into a higher observed category. The model also includes response noise, so $P(s_{ijk}>0)$ is distinct from the probability that the observed treatment response exceeds the control potential response. Its interpretation as favorable affect depends on the measurement model and the meaning of the endorsement cue.

I fit the baseline model without covariates using \texttt{endorse}.\footnote{The fitting script uses three chains with starting values dispersed across negative, zero, and positive mean reactions. Each chain runs for 200,000 iterations, discards 20,000, and retains every 40th draw. The largest reported Gelman--Rubin statistic across the mean and variance parameters is \MaxRhat{}, and the smallest effective sample size across mean reactions is \MinESS{}. The fits use the package's default priors.}
% MCMC settings above are fixed inputs in R/model.R, not estimated results.
\Cref{tab:endorse_results} reports posterior means and credible intervals. Averaged equally over groups, the probability of a positive latent reaction is \PrimaryShare{} (95\% credible interval \PrimaryInterval{}). This is an average of four marginal probabilities, not the probability of favoring at least one group. The Afghan Taliban have the lowest estimate, \AfghanShare{}, and a wide interval, \AfghanInterval{}. Their posterior probability of a negative mean reaction is \AfghanNegativeProbability{}; the other groups' mean-reaction intervals include zero.

\section{Within-Respondent Patterns}
\label{sec:within}

Each respondent answers all four policy questions. Endorsed respondents receive a different group on each question; control respondents receive none. Random assignment therefore gives the control arm's joint distribution of the four answers as the counterfactual for the endorsed arm. Two features of that joint distribution bear on the measurement model: how a respondent's answers move together across policies, and the distribution of each respondent's mean answer. Neither requires a model. The \MixedCount{} respondents with both control and endorsed items are excluded from this section; intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals over respondents.

In the measurement model, reactions are independent across questions for the same respondent, so the correlation between any two policy answers is the same with and without an endorsement, apart from the effect of the mean shift on the discrete scale. \Cref{tab:within_correlation} shows that the correlations change, and not in one direction. The two policies whose answers are most strongly related in the control arm, Frontier Crimes Regulation reform and the Durand Line, become less related under endorsement: the correlation falls from \StrongPairControl{} to \StrongPairEndorsed{} (\StrongPairChange{}, 95\% interval \StrongPairInterval{}). Every other pair becomes more related, by \WeakPairChangeMin{} to \WeakPairChangeMax{}, with every interval excluding zero. A reaction that each respondent carries across all four endorsements raises every pairwise correlation; a cue that also loosens the link between an answer and the respondent's policy position lowers the correlation for the pair most tightly linked to that position. The pattern is consistent with both operating together. It is not consistent with reactions that are independent across questions, and it is not consistent with reactions that enter each question in proportion to that question's loading on the latent position, which would raise the FCR--Durand correlation most.

This comparison holds the counterfactual fixed at the observed control arm and varies only the direction of reactions. It assumes that whether and how a respondent reacts is unrelated to where their answers would otherwise sit. Without that assumption the marginal distributions alone guarantee only that some respondents moved down: a sharp lower bound on the share whose mean answer fell is \MeanFallBound{}, while the corresponding bound for a rise is \MeanRiseBound{}, because a reaction that lowers only the answers of respondents just above the midpoint could refill it. The comparison also does not exclude every mechanism that moves answers in both directions. An endorsement could, for example, push respondents off the middle of the scale toward their own policy views without any of them reacting to the group; that mechanism also empties the midpoint. What the comparison excludes is the reading under which the small average effect comes from a group that reacts in one direction while the rest do not react at all.

These patterns do not identify a proportion of favorable reactions. They establish that endorsements produce reactions that respondents carry across questions, that those reactions are large relative to the average effect, and that where movement in both directions is observable it occurs in both directions. The small average effect reflects opposing reactions that offset, not an absence of reactions.

\section{Behavioral Assumptions and Supporter Proportions}
\label{sec:framework}

The measurement model estimates a latent distribution under parametric assumptions. A simpler decomposition shows why the ATE alone cannot identify a supporter proportion. Suppose a proportion $p$ holds favorable attitudes and the remainder holds unfavorable attitudes. Let $\delta^+$ and $\delta^-$ be their average effects on the observed response. Then
\begin{align}
\beta=p\delta^+ +(1-p)\delta^-.
\label{eq:ate_decomposition}
\end{align}
Interpreting the two classes as supporters and opponents requires behavioral assumptions, such as $\delta^+>0$ and $\delta^-<0$. One observed mean effect cannot determine the three unknowns. This is a partial-identification problem \citep{manski1990nonparametric}. The control mean $\alpha$ supplies information about baseline responses, but does not resolve the decomposition without restrictions on how baseline preferences relate to attitudes and effects.

\subsection{Binary Switching and Baseline Information}

Suppose every supporter ends at 1 and every opponent at 0 under treatment. Then the treatment mean directly identifies $p$:
\begin{align}
p=E[Y(1)]=\alpha+\beta.
\label{eq:binary_switching}
\end{align}
This endpoint restriction identifies $p$ without separately identifying both class-specific baseline means. Applied to the pooled data it gives \BinaryShare{}. But the restriction also predicts treatment variance $p(1-p)$ and excludes all interior responses. The observed interior categories contradict this model.

The baseline still matters. Reporting $\alpha$ and $\beta$ reveals both treatment and control means, whereas reporting only $\beta$ discards information about the scale's available room for movement. The control mean of \BaselineMean{} is a mean intensity, not a supporter fraction. For comparison, \TopTwoShare{} of control responses occupy the top two categories.

Partial awareness requires another assumption. If a fraction $q$ has an opinion and follows binary switching, while the rest is unaffected with baseline mean $a_U$, then
\begin{align}
p_{\mathrm{aware}}=\frac{\alpha+\beta-(1-q)a_U}{q}.
\end{align}
The expression $\alpha+\beta/q$ follows only if the unaffected subgroup has baseline mean $\alpha$. With $q=0.7$ as an illustrative assumption, allowing feasible subgroup means gives a range from \AwareLower{} to 100\%, rather than a unique estimate near the full-sample treatment mean. This calculation remains conditional on binary switching among opinion-holders.

\subsection{Alternative Moment Restrictions}

To illustrate sensitivity to assumptions, I hold the pooled ATE fixed and vary the restrictions on endorsement effects.

\textbf{Symmetric class-average effects.} If $\delta^+=d$ and $\delta^-=-d$, then
\begin{align}
p=\frac{\beta}{2d}+\frac{1}{2}, \qquad |\beta|\leq d.
\end{align}
Using the pooled ATE, $d=0.05$ implies \SymmetricShare{}, whereas $d=0.02$ implies \SmallSymmetricShare{}. These choices constrain class-average effects; they do not require every individual to move by a noninteger number of response categories. Neither value of $d$ is identified by the pooled ATE and baseline alone.

\textbf{Extreme opponent reactions.} If opponents move from 1 to 0 and everyone else is unaffected, then $\beta=-(1-p)$, so $p=1+\beta$, or \ExtremeShare{}. Here $p$ is the unaffected fraction. Calling it a supporter fraction requires an additional classification assumption; the calculation supplies no positive reactions. The restriction also implies $\beta\leq0$ and specific changes in the endpoint masses.

\textbf{A continuous normal approximation.} If one approximates individual effects by $\delta_i\sim\mathcal{N}(\beta,\sigma^2)$, then $P(\delta_i>0)=\Phi(\beta/\sigma)$. Setting $\sigma=0.05$ gives \NormalShare{}. An exact normal distribution is incompatible with differences on a bounded five-point scale. This calculation is a continuous approximation, not a fitted ordinal model. It differs from \cref{eq:latent_probability}, where normality applies to a latent reaction rather than an observed response difference.

\subsection{Ceiling Effects on the Discrete Scale}

The response ceiling can attenuate upward movement. In the control group, \CeilingShare{} of responses are at 1. A respondent at that endpoint cannot report greater policy support, although a respondent with an unfavorable reaction can move down. This is a measurement constraint, not bias in an ATE defined on the observed response scale.

For an illustration, suppose supporters and opponents share the observed control distribution, and each moves up or down by the same number of categories, subject to the endpoints. Let $\bar\delta^+$ and $\bar\delta^-$ denote the resulting average shifts. Solving \cref{eq:ate_decomposition} gives \DiscreteOneShare{} for a one-category shift, \DiscreteTwoShare{} for two, and \DiscreteThreeShare{} for three (\cref{tab:discrete_scale}). The baseline distribution within attitude classes is unobserved; compatibility of the resulting treatment distribution with the data would require a separate check. The calculations show how truncation can produce a negative mean despite a majority assigned to the favorable class.

\subsection{Bounds on Observed Response Increases}

A different calculation bounds observed response changes without specifying a latent reaction distribution. Under valid randomization, observation, and consistency assumptions, the experiment identifies treatment and control marginal outcome distributions, but not their joint distribution. Allowing every coupling of the estimated pooled marginals gives bounds of \LowerBound{} to \UpperBound{} on the fraction of person--policy responses for which $Y(1)>Y(0)$ (\cref{tab:pairing_bounds}). These are plug-in bounds conditional on the empirical marginals, not confidence limits. Pooling also discards policy-specific restrictions on the joint distribution.

Independent random pairing yields a higher treatment response \RandomHigher{} of the time, a lower one \RandomLower{}, and a tie \RandomEqual{}. Independence is an additional assumption about the coupling, not a feature identified by the experiment. The bounds permit either a minority or a majority of observed responses to increase. They do not bound the fraction holding favorable attitudes or the latent probability in \cref{sec:model}.

\subsection{Comparison with Direct Polling}

% Polling percentages: Pew Research Center (2012), Chapter 2 and topline, Afghan Taliban.
Pew's 2012 survey reports 14\% favorable views of the Afghan Taliban, 45\% unfavorable views, and 41\% with no opinion \citep{pew2012attitudes}. If stated opinions are classified correctly, assigning all nonrespondents to the favorable category gives an upper bound of 55\%. That bound does not allow respondents who report unfavorable opinions to conceal favorable ones.

The corresponding Afghan Taliban estimate from the 2009 endorsement data is \AfghanShare{} (95\% credible interval \AfghanInterval{}). It falls within the range obtained by varying the classification of Pew nonrespondents. But the surveys differ in year, sample composition, and measurement, and the latent probability is not the same response as direct favorability. Social desirability and fear may contribute to discrepancies; these data do not identify their contribution.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Researchers should distinguish the average effect on an observed response, the fraction of responses with positive effects, and the prevalence of favorable attitudes. Random assignment identifies the first under the usual design assumptions. Marginal response distributions can bound the second. Estimating the third requires a model connecting responses to attitudes, and its credibility depends on that connection.

Reporting treatment and control distributions alongside the ATE makes such assumptions easier to assess. Endpoint restrictions can be checked against interior responses; ceiling constraints are visible in the baseline distribution. Model-based positive probabilities should be reported with credible intervals, the population over which they are averaged, and evidence about model fit and sensitivity to priors.

The within-respondent patterns in \cref{sec:within} are the first such check this paper applies, and the baseline measurement model does not pass it: the model implies unchanged correlations across policy answers, and the data show correlations that rise for most pairs and fall for one. A model in which a reaction shifts a respondent's answers directly, rather than through the latent position, is the natural alternative; estimating the proportion of favorable reactions under it is left for future work. The present reanalysis also leaves open population adjustment, Bayesian sensitivity to the assignment exclusions, prior sensitivity, and posterior predictive fit of the baseline model. Endorsement credibility is another measurement question. If respondents disbelieve a cue or infer information beyond the endorser's identity, the mapping to attitudes can change; the direction of that change is not established by the ATE alone.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A negative average endorsement effect does not establish how widely unfavorable attitudes are held. In the unweighted analysis sample, the fitted measurement model implies a \PrimaryShare{} probability of a positive latent reaction, averaged across four endorsers, with a 95\% credible interval of \PrimaryInterval{}. Moving from an average response effect to a distribution of attitudes requires stating and defending the additional assumptions.
