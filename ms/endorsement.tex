\begin{abstract}
\noindent \citet{blair2013poverty} conclude that Pakistanis hold militant groups in ``low regard.'' Their data support that conclusion more firmly once a fieldwork problem is set aside. Three of the survey's \InterviewerCount{} interviewers recorded the same four answers in most of their interviews, top support for two policies and none for the other two, and in interviews with militant endorsements they recorded one of those answers a notch higher. No other interviewer recorded the first pattern in more than \OtherBlockMax{} of interviews without endorsements. Their \SuspectInterviews{} interviews, \SuspectShare{} of the total, make respondents look warmer toward militant groups than the rest of the sample is. Without them, the average effect of an endorsement on policy support grows from \FullATEPoints{} to \PooledATEPoints{} points on a 0--100 scale. The standard measurement model's estimate of the share who react favorably moves from \FullPrimaryShare{} to \PrimaryShare{}, a change well within the uncertainty of either estimate. The three interviewers also produce the published data's appearance that endorsements push respondents out of the middle of the scale in both directions, the pattern offsetting favorable and unfavorable reactions would produce. Without them, that pattern disappears. What remains fits opposition alone but does not rule out a favorable minority, so the model's favorable share rests on its assumption that reactions are normally distributed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Do Pakistanis hold militant groups in ``low regard''? \citet{blair2013poverty} fielded a 6,000-person endorsement experiment to study how poverty relates to support for four militant groups, using a design that sidesteps the nonresponse and social desirability problems of direct questions. Their central finding is that poor Pakistanis dislike militant groups more than middle-class Pakistanis do. Along the way they report that an endorsement by any of the four groups lowers support for a policy, and they conclude that Pakistanis in general are weakly negative toward these organizations. The effect is small. In their replication data, support falls by \FullATEPoints{} points on a 0--100 scale when a militant group is said to back the policy. Being associated with al-Qa'ida or the Afghan Taliban appears to cost a policy almost nothing.

Their data support that conclusion more firmly than estimates from all interviews do, once a fieldwork problem is set aside. In most of their interviews without endorsements, interviewers 7, 37, and 71 recorded the same four answers: top support for polio vaccination and curriculum reform, none for Frontier Crimes Regulation reform or the Durand Line jirga. In most of their interviews with endorsements, they recorded the same answers with Frontier Crimes Regulation support one notch higher. No other interviewer of the survey's \InterviewerCount{} recorded either combination often (\cref{fig:interviewers}). The three recorded \SuspectInterviews{} interviews, \SuspectShare{} of the total. Because their endorsed interviews show more support than their unendorsed ones, they make militant endorsements look less costly. Without them, the average effect grows from \FullATEPoints{} to \PooledATEPoints{} points. Under the measurement model of \citet{bullock2011statistical}, the estimated probability that a respondent reacts favorably to an endorsement moves from \FullPrimaryShare{} (95\% credible interval \FullPrimaryInterval{}) with their interviews to \PrimaryShare{} (\PrimaryInterval{}) without them, a change much smaller than the uncertainty in either estimate. The effect remains small: averaged over the four groups, a militant endorsement still costs a policy about two points.

The three interviewers matter more for a second question: whether the small average hides large reactions in both directions. A small average has three readings. Most respondents could dislike the groups mildly; most could be indifferent; or large numbers could like and dislike the groups, with reactions that nearly cancel. Because each respondent answers four policy questions, the design can separate them: a respondent who reacts to militant endorsements reacts on all four, which moves the average of their four answers well away from where it would otherwise be. With all interviews, respondents whose answers average the middle of the scale become \FullMidpointDrop{} percentage points rarer under endorsement, and the positions just above and just below both gain, as large offsetting reactions would produce. Once standard errors account for interviewers, even that drop is not distinguishable from zero. The three interviewers' answers average exactly the middle of the scale without endorsements and just above it with them, and they account for all of the movement out of the middle and most of the movement above it. Without them, the share at the middle no longer falls and the position just above no longer gains. Simulations built from respondents who saw no endorsements show that opposition alone fits what remains, and so do simulations in which up to \TwoSidedTopFavour{} react favorably.

How many respondents react favorably is therefore not identified by the data. The measurement model's share comes from its assumption that reactions are normally distributed around a small negative mean, and nothing model-free in these data confirms or rules out a favorable minority of that size. The analysis does not revisit Blair et al.'s income comparison, which rests on the same interviews.

\section{Endorsement Experiments}
\label{sec:background}

Endorsement experiments randomize whether a policy question mentions that an actor of interest supports the policy. Respondents in the control condition evaluate the policy alone; those in the treatment condition evaluate it with the endorsement. The difference in average support identifies the effect of the cue on stated support. Reading that effect as affect toward the endorser requires that respondents understand the cue and that it works through their attitudes toward the group. The design has been used to study militant groups in Pakistan and combatants in Afghanistan \citep{bullock2011statistical, blair2013poverty, lyall2013explaining, blair2014comparing}.

A small negative average treatment effect can reflect many small negative reactions, a few large ones, or positive and negative reactions that nearly cancel. Suppose a proportion $p$ of respondents reacts favorably and the rest unfavorably, with average effects $\delta^+$ and $\delta^-$ on the observed response. Then
\begin{align}
\beta=p\delta^+ +(1-p)\delta^-.
\label{eq:ate_decomposition}
\end{align}
One observed mean cannot determine three unknowns \citep{manski1990nonparametric}. Mild disapproval ($p$ near zero, small negative $\delta^-$), indifference ($\delta^+$ and $\delta^-$ near zero), and offsetting reactions (large $\delta^+$ and $\delta^-$ of opposite sign) can all produce the same $\beta$. When each respondent answers several questions, the joint distribution of their answers carries more information: a reaction carried across questions moves a respondent's average answer further than independent noise on each answer does.

\section{The Experiment and Data}
\label{sec:data}

% Survey date and original sample sizes: Blair et al. (2013), survey design and Table 1.
\citet{blair2013poverty} surveyed 6,000 adults in Pakistan's four main provinces in 2009. Respondents rated polio vaccination, Frontier Crimes Regulation reform, the Durand Line jirga, and madrassa curriculum reform on a five-point scale from ``not at all'' to ``a great deal.'' Respondents in the endorsement condition heard a different group endorse each policy: Kashmiri tanzeems, the Afghan Taliban, al-Qa'ida, and sectarian tanzeems. I rescale answers to $[0,1]$, so that each step on the scale is 0.25; a mean on this scale is not a percentage of respondents supporting a policy.

I use Blair et al.'s replication file \citep{blair2013replication}, which records each respondent's interviewer, district, and province. The analysis sample has \FullSampleSize{} respondents who gave a substantive answer to all four policies and have a recorded interviewer; \MixedCount{} of them received endorsements on some policies but not others and are excluded from the within-respondent comparisons. The \texttt{endorse} R package distributes a version of the same data without interviewer identifiers \citep{shiraito2022endorse}. The analysis is unweighted. Blair et al. use population and policy weights, and their survey oversampled Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, so the estimates below describe the sample rather than Pakistan.

Endorsement versions were assigned within interviewers, and the \InterviewerCount{} interviewers recorded between \MinInterviews{} and \MaxInterviews{} interviews each (median \MedianInterviews{}). Answers recorded by the same interviewer tend to resemble one another more than answers recorded by different interviewers \citep{west2017interviewer}. Standard errors for the average effect are therefore clustered by interviewer, and every bootstrap interval resamples interviewers, keeping each interviewer's respondents in both conditions together.

\section{Three Interviewers}
\label{sec:interviewers}

Every interviewer conducted interviews in both versions, so each yields an estimate of the treatment effect. If interviewers differed only by sampling noise, those estimates would scatter around the pooled effect by no more than their standard errors allow. They do not. Among the \HeterogeneityTested{} interviewers with at least 20 respondents and some variation in recorded answers, Cochran's $Q$, the inverse-variance weighted sum of squared deviations of the interviewer effects from their weighted mean, is \HeterogeneityStat{} on \HeterogeneityDF{} degrees of freedom ($p=\HeterogeneityP{}$). \Cref{tab:interviewer_effects} lists the interviewers furthest from the pooled effect. Interviewers 71, 37, and 7 rank \SuspectZRanks{}, with effects of \SuspectEffectMin{} to \SuspectEffectMax{} points: in their interviews, an endorsement by a militant group raises support. Leaving one interviewer out at a time, removing interviewer 37 moves the pooled effect more than removing any other (the three rank \SuspectShiftRanks{}). Without the three, the interviewer effects are consistent with sampling noise (\HeterogeneityStatClean{} on \HeterogeneityDFClean{} degrees of freedom, $p=\HeterogeneityPClean{}$). Another \ZeroVarianceCount{} interviewers recorded identical answers on the four policies in every interview, so their effects have no sampling variance at all.

Whether these interviewers filled in forms themselves, coached respondents, or recorded answers in some other way cannot be told from the data. Duplicated answer strings are a known sign of fabricated interviews \citep{kuriakose2016duped}, but the question here is narrower. Answers this uniform do not behave like independent responses, and they produce exactly the features of the data that the reading of offsetting reactions rests on.

The 4-0-0-4 combination averages 8/16, the middle of the scale, and 4-1-0-4 averages 9/16, one step above it. Of the \MidpointCountControl{} respondents without endorsements whose answers average the middle of the scale, \MidpointCountSuspect{} were interviewed by the three; of the \MidpointCountEndorsed{} with endorsements, \MidpointCountEndorsedSuspect{} were. Their shift from one combination to the other moves respondents out of the middle and into the position just above it, and it makes their endorsed interviews record more support than their unendorsed ones. \Cref{tab:interviewer_samples} shows the consequences. With all interviews, the share of respondents averaging the middle falls by \FullMidpointDrop{} points under endorsement. With the interviewer-clustered interval, even that change is not distinguishable from zero (\FullMidpointChange{}). Without the three interviewers, the share at the middle barely changes and the gain one step above disappears, while the gain one step below remains. The average effect grows from $\FullATE{}$ to $\PooledATE{}$. Dropping every interviewer flagged by the repetition screen, or the interviewers with duplicated interviews (\cref{sec:quality}), leaves the changes around the middle much as they are without the three, and the average effect grows further, to $\UnflaggedATE{}$ and $\UnduplicatedATE{}$.

\section{Data Quality beyond the Three}
\label{sec:quality}

The three interviewers' answers to the rest of the questionnaire are uniform but not copied: no two of their interviews match on every question. The survey has a broader problem. For each respondent, the share of questions on which the most similar other respondent in the same sampling point gave identical answers is a standard check for duplicated or fabricated interviews \citep{kuriakose2016duped}. Across the \QuestionnaireItems{} closed-ended questions asked of at least half the respondents, the median of that best match is \MatchMedian{}; \MatchEightyFive{} of respondents have a match on at least 85\% of questions, \MatchNinety{} on at least 90\%, and \MatchTwins{} have an exact twin. The \DuplicatePairs{} identical pairs come from \DuplicatingInterviewers{} interviewers, most from interviewers \TopDuplicators{} (\TopDuplicatorPairs{} pairs).

How high best matches run depends on how many questions there are, how many answer options they offer, and how alike respondents are \citep{simmons2016duped}. Most of these questions are binary, and respondents in a sampling point are neighbors. As a benchmark, I drew 200 sets of 92 questions from the face-to-face interviews of the 2012 American National Election Study, matching the number of answer options of the Pakistan questions; the sets agree by chance slightly more often than the Pakistan questions do.\footnote{Questions were drawn from the 514 interviewer-administered items asked of at least half of the 2,054 face-to-face respondents, with don't know and refusal kept as answers. Because the study has only 25 binary items, binary items were drawn with replacement, which raises matches. Scripts for both benchmarks are in \texttt{exploration/duplicate-benchmarks}; neither survey's data are redistributed.} Within the study's primary sampling units, which average about 16 face-to-face respondents, the median best match is about 70\%, about 0.1\% of respondents match another on at least 85\% of questions, and no set produces an exact twin. Pew's 2009 survey of Pakistan, fielded in the same year by a different firm and recording interviewer identifiers, is a closer benchmark \citep{pew2009data}. Across its 142 closed-ended questions, the median best match among its 1,254 Pakistani respondents is 69\%, 3\% match another respondent on at least 85\% of questions, and none has an exact twin. Pew asked more questions, which lowers best matches, but each Pew respondent is compared with the whole national sample rather than with eleven neighbors, which raises them. Near-duplicates are far more common in Blair et al.'s survey than in either benchmark: \MatchEightyFive{} of respondents match a neighbor on at least 85\% of questions, against 3\% in Pew's survey and 0.1\% in the American study, and exact twins occur in neither benchmark. Excluding the duplicating interviewers along with the three leaves the average effect at $\UnduplicatedATE{}$ (\cref{tab:interviewer_samples}).

\label{sec:within}

If reactions were independent across a respondent's questions, the correlation between answers to two policies would be the same with and without endorsements, apart from small changes the mean shift causes on a discrete scale. \section{How Many React Favorably?}
\label{sec:model}

The within-respondent patterns do not say how many respondents react favorably. Each route to that number rests on assumptions the data cannot check.

\subsection{The Measurement Model}

The measurement model of \citet{bullock2011statistical} relates ordinal policy answers to a latent policy position $x_i$ for each respondent and a reaction $s_{ijk}$ to group $k$'s endorsement of question $j$, which shifts the position before it is mapped to an answer through question-specific loadings and cutpoints. In the baseline specification,
\begin{align}
s_{ijk} &\sim \mathcal{N}(\lambda_k,\omega_k^2), &
P(s_{ijk}>0) &= \Phi(\lambda_k/\omega_k).
\label{eq:latent_probability}
\end{align}
The mean and variance of the reaction are common across policies, and each reaction is drawn independently across a respondent's questions. A positive latent reaction need not move an answer into a higher category, so $P(s_{ijk}>0)$ is not the share of answers an endorsement raises.

I fit the baseline model with \texttt{endorse}, with and without the three interviewers.\footnote{Each fit uses three chains with starting values dispersed across negative, zero, and positive mean reactions. Each chain runs for 200,000 iterations, discards 20,000, and retains every 40th draw. The largest Gelman--Rubin statistic across the mean and variance parameters in either fit is \MaxRhat{}, and the smallest effective sample size across mean reactions is \MinESS{}. The fits use the package's default priors.}
% MCMC settings above are fixed inputs in R/model.R, not estimated results.
Without them, the probability of a positive latent reaction, averaged equally over the four groups, is \PrimaryShare{} (95\% credible interval \PrimaryInterval{}); with all interviews it is \FullPrimaryShare{}. \Cref{tab:endorse_results} reports the estimates by group. The Afghan Taliban have the lowest estimate, \AfghanShare{} (\AfghanInterval{}). For comparison, in Pew's 2009 survey of Pakistan, 10\% of respondents said they viewed the Taliban favorably and 20\% gave no answer \citep{pew2009data}, though a stated opinion and a latent reaction to an endorsement are different quantities. The model's estimate of how many react favorably comes from its normal reaction distribution and its independence assumption, not from features of the data checked here.

\subsection{Bounds and Restrictions}

Without a model, the experiment identifies the distributions of answers with and without endorsements but not how they pair within a person. Allowing every pairing bounds the fraction of answers an endorsement raises between \LowerBound{} and \UpperBound{}.

Fixing the sizes of favorable and unfavorable effects in \cref{eq:ate_decomposition} turns the average effect into a share. \Cref{tab:method_comparison} collects several such restrictions. The implied shares run from \SmallSymmetricShare{} to \ExtremeShare{}. The response ceiling widens the range: \CeilingShare{} of answers without endorsements are at the top of the scale, where a favorable reaction cannot show. If favorable and unfavorable respondents both move one category, truncation at the top implies a favorable share of \DiscreteOneShare{} despite a negative mean.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Interviewer effects are a standard concern in survey research \citep{west2017interviewer}, and they bear on endorsement experiments in two ways. First, randomizing the questionnaire version within interviewers does not protect the treatment effect from them. An interviewer who records a fixed answer string under each version adds a fixed difference to the estimated effect, and here the three interviewers' differences had the opposite sign from the pooled effect and were among the largest in the survey. Second, analyses that use the joint distribution of a respondent's answers are especially exposed, because a repeated answer string is a joint pattern. Intervals that resample respondents treat an interviewer's repeated strings as independent evidence; resampling interviewers does not, and on its own it makes the published midpoint pattern indistinguishable from zero. A test of whether the effect varies across interviewers more than chance allows needs nothing beyond the identifiers, and it finds the three without being told where to look.

The checks used here come from work on detecting fabricated and shortcut interviews. Interviewers who invent answers tend to record answer strings that vary too little within an interview and are too alike across interviews \citep{schraepler2005characteristics,menold2014real,blasius2015should}, and screens built on such indicators can single out falsifiers from the data alone \citep{bredl2012statistical,schwanhaeuser2022catch}. Survey organizations pair these screens with timestamps, callbacks, and reinterviews \citep{murphy2016interviewer,dematteis2020falsification}. Percent match applies the logic to whole interviews \citep{kuriakose2016duped}, and its cutoffs depend on the questionnaire and the sample \citep{simmons2016duped}. The same idea, that invented data are too regular, has exposed teachers who altered answer sheets \citep{jacob2003rotten} and fabricated experimental data \citep{simonsohn2013just,broockman2015irregularities}. Such checks locate problems; they do not establish fabrication. Here the evidence stops short of it: the three interviewers' answers to the rest of the questionnaire are uniform but not copied. What the checks establish is narrower and enough for the argument: the patterns that suggested offsetting reactions depend on three interviewers.

The favorable share needs a different design. Randomizing whether the group is said to support or oppose the policy separates reactions to the group from a general push away from the middle of the scale: a respondent who favors the group should move in opposite directions under the two cues. Policies with intermediate baseline support, or a finer response scale, leave room for favorable reactions to appear; here \CeilingShare{} of control answers are already at the top. Asking the policy questions without endorsements in an earlier wave would observe each respondent's own shift. Repeated-measures designs in survey experiments have generally not altered treatment effects \citep{clifford2021increasing}, although consistency pressure across waves would need to be checked.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Three of \InterviewerCount{} interviewers in Blair et al.'s survey recorded the same answer strings in most of their interviews, and their interviews make Pakistanis look warmer toward militant groups than the rest of the sample is. Without them, the average effect of an endorsement grows from \FullATEPoints{} to \PooledATEPoints{} points on a 0--100 scale, while the measurement model's share of favorable reactions moves from \FullPrimaryShare{} to \PrimaryShare{}, within the uncertainty of either estimate. They also produce the appearance that endorsements push respondents out of the middle of the scale in both directions. Without them, that appearance goes away, and what remains fits opposition alone without ruling out a favorable minority. Pakistanis in this survey hold these groups in lower regard than estimates from all interviews imply, although whether a sizable favorable minority exists is not settled by these data.
