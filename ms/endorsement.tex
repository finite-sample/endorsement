\begin{abstract}
\noindent \citet{blair2013poverty} conclude that Pakistanis hold militant groups in ``low regard.'' Their evidence says something more striking. When the Afghan Taliban, al-Qa'ida, or Kashmiri or sectarian militant groups are said to back a policy, support for it falls by just \PooledATEPoints{} points on a 0--100 scale, an effect the authors call substantively small. Being associated with these groups costs a policy almost nothing. In ordinary usage low regard means a dim view, but an effect this small could also mean that most Pakistanis are indifferent to the groups or only mildly negative. Or it could mean that large numbers like and dislike them and the reactions nearly cancel. Because each respondent answers four policy questions, the data show whether endorsement moves individual people up or down across the board. It does both: respondents whose four answers average to the middle of the scale become \MidpointDrop{} percentage points rarer, and the positions just above and just below both gain. Mild disapproval or a minority of opponents would fill only the lower side, and simulations built from control respondents confirm that opposition alone leaves the middle unchanged. A sizable share reacts favorably: the standard measurement model puts it at \PrimaryShare{}, and a simulated world with \TwoSidedTopFavour{} favorable and \TwoSidedTopOppose{} opposed produces only about half the observed drop at the midpoint. The exact share is not identified, because the data fix how much answers move but not whether many respondents move a little or fewer move a lot.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Do Pakistanis hold militant groups in ``low regard''? \citet{blair2013poverty} fielded a 6,000-person endorsement experiment to study how poverty relates to support for four militant groups, using a design that sidesteps the nonresponse and social desirability problems of direct questions. Their central finding is that poor Pakistanis dislike militant groups more than middle-class Pakistanis do. Along the way they report a result that deserves more attention than it has received. When Kashmiri tanzeems, the Afghan Taliban, al-Qa'ida, or sectarian tanzeems are said to back a policy, support for it falls by just \PooledATEPoints{} points on a 0--100 scale, an effect they call substantively small. Being associated with these groups costs a policy almost nothing. They read the small negative average as showing that Pakistanis in general are weakly negative toward these organizations, which is the natural reading. But the same average could come from indifference, or from large favorable and unfavorable reactions that nearly cancel. The analysis here asks which, and does not bear on their income comparison. It is possible because of their design: each respondent answers four policy questions.

The small average effect reflects offsetting reactions, not widespread mild disapproval or indifference. Because each respondent answers four policy questions, the control arm supplies the counterfactual for how a respondent's answers move together, not only for each answer separately. Under endorsement, answers to five of the six policy pairs become more closely related, which reactions carried across questions produce and independent noise does not. Respondents whose four answers average the midpoint of the scale become \MidpointDrop{} percentage points rarer, with gains on both sides. Endorsed arms simulated from the control respondents show that opposition alone, at each of three intensities that reproduce the average effect, leaves that share unchanged (\cref{sec:within}). The argument assumes that how a respondent reacts is unrelated to where their answers would otherwise sit.

The measurement model of \citet{bullock2011statistical} gives a \PrimaryShare{} probability of a positive latent reaction to an endorsement, averaged equally across the four groups (95\% credible interval \PrimaryInterval{}), with mean reactions no larger than \MaxMeanReaction{} ideal-point standard deviations and larger estimated dispersion. That model treats each reaction as independent across a respondent's questions, and the within-respondent patterns contradict it. The data show that substantial favorable and unfavorable reactions coexist; they do not pin down how many respondents react favorably.

\section{Endorsement Experiments}
\label{sec:background}

Endorsement experiments randomize whether a policy question includes an endorsement by an actor of interest. Respondents in the control condition evaluate the policy alone; those in the treatment condition evaluate the policy with the endorsement. With valid assignment and observation, the difference identifies an effect of the cue on stated policy support.

Interpreting that effect as affect toward the endorser requires more. Respondents must understand the endorsement, and its effect must operate through their attitudes toward the group rather than through other information the cue conveys. Even then, the size of a response change can depend on baseline policy preferences and the response scale. The design has been used to study militant groups in Pakistan and combatants in Afghanistan \citep{bullock2011statistical, blair2013poverty, lyall2013explaining, blair2014comparing}.

A small negative ATE can reflect many small negative reactions, a few large ones, or positive and negative reactions that nearly cancel. Suppose a proportion $p$ of respondents reacts favorably and the rest unfavorably, with average effects $\delta^+$ and $\delta^-$ on the observed response. Then
\begin{align}
\beta=p\delta^+ +(1-p)\delta^-.
\label{eq:ate_decomposition}
\end{align}
One observed mean cannot determine three unknowns \citep{manski1990nonparametric}. Widespread mild disapproval ($p$ near zero, small negative $\delta^-$), indifference ($\delta^+$ and $\delta^-$ near zero), and offsetting reactions (large $\delta^+$ and $\delta^-$ of opposite sign) can all produce the same $\beta$. Nor does the sign of an effect, without a measurement assumption, classify a respondent as a supporter or opponent. Each respondent in this experiment answers several questions, and the way those answers move together separates the two readings (\cref{sec:within}).

\section{The Experiment and Data}
\label{sec:data}

% Survey date and original sample sizes: Blair et al. (2013), survey design and Table 1.
\citet{blair2013poverty} surveyed 6,000 adults in Pakistan's four main provinces in 2009. Their experiment covered Kashmiri tanzeems, the Afghan Taliban, al-Qa'ida, and sectarian tanzeems. Respondents evaluated polio vaccination, Frontier Crimes Regulation reform, the Durand Line, and curriculum standardization on a five-point intensity scale. I rescale responses to $[0,1]$; a mean on this scale is not a percentage of respondents supporting a policy.

I use the analysis file supplied with the \texttt{endorse} R package \citep{shiraito2022endorse}. It contains \SampleSize{} respondents with one observed answer per policy, yielding \ControlCount{} control responses and \TreatmentCount{} endorsed responses. The pooled control mean is \BaselineMean{}, the treatment mean is \TreatmentMean{}, and their difference is $\PooledATE{}$ (respondent-clustered SE \PooledSE{}). These are response-weighted means across the available policy items and endorsers.

% Original weighting and estimates: Blair et al. (2013), survey design, footnote 31, Table 1.
The analysis is unweighted. Blair et al. use population and policy weights; their survey oversampled Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Their unconditional Table 1 has 5,358 respondents and a control mean of 0.796. Differences from the present estimates can reflect sample selection and policy weighting as well as population weighting. The estimates below therefore describe the analysis sample, not nationally representative prevalence.

The package sample also retains assignment patterns excluded by the original replication code: \MixedCount{} respondents have control and endorsed items across policies, and \RepeatedCount{} receive the same endorser on multiple policies. Excluding these \AnomalyCount{} respondents leaves the pooled ATE at $\CleanATE{}$, the same to three decimals as in the full sample. The Bayesian models retain the package sample; their sensitivity to these exclusions has not been estimated.

\section{Within-Respondent Patterns}
\label{sec:within}

Each respondent answers all four policy questions. Endorsed respondents receive a different group on each question; control respondents receive none. Random assignment therefore gives the control arm's joint distribution of the four answers as the counterfactual for the endorsed arm. Two features of that joint distribution show whether respondents react: how a respondent's answers move together across policies, and the distribution of each respondent's mean answer. Neither requires a model. The \MixedCount{} respondents with both control and endorsed items are excluded from this section; intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals over respondents.

If reactions are independent across a respondent's questions, as in the standard measurement model \citep{bullock2011statistical}, the correlation between any two policy answers is the same with and without an endorsement, apart from the effect of the mean shift on the discrete scale. \Cref{tab:within_correlation} shows that the correlations change, and not in one direction. The two policies whose answers are most strongly related in the control arm, Frontier Crimes Regulation reform and the Durand Line, become less related under endorsement: the correlation falls from \StrongPairControl{} to \StrongPairEndorsed{} (\StrongPairChange{}, 95\% interval \StrongPairInterval{}). Every other pair becomes more related, by \WeakPairChangeMin{} to \WeakPairChangeMax{}, with every interval excluding zero. A reaction that each respondent carries across all four endorsements raises every pairwise correlation; a cue that also loosens the link between an answer and the respondent's policy position lowers the correlation for the pair most tightly linked to that position. The pattern is consistent with both operating together. It is not consistent with reactions that are independent across questions, and it is not consistent with reactions that enter each question in proportion to that question's loading on the latent position, which would raise the FCR--Durand correlation most.

The most direct alternative to offsetting reactions is a minority that reacts strongly against the endorser while everyone else is unmoved. That alternative also produces a small average effect and greater dispersion. To see whether it can produce the midpoint pattern, \cref{tab:reaction_worlds} builds endorsed arms from the control respondents themselves. In each simulated arm, a reacting respondent moves each of their four answers one step in their direction with a fixed probability, and the share of opponents is chosen so the expected average effect equals the observed one. Opposition alone, held by \OneSidedOpposeMin{} to \OneSidedOpposeMax{} of respondents depending on how many answers move, leaves the midpoint share where it was (changes of \OneSidedMidpointMin{} to \OneSidedMidpointMax{} points). Respondents leaving the midpoint downward are replaced by respondents arriving from above. In 97.5\% of simulated samples of the observed size, opposition alone lowers the midpoint share by less than \OneSidedMidpointLowest{} points; the observed change is \MidpointChange{}. Adding a favorable group empties the midpoint: with \TwoSidedTopFavour{} favorable and \TwoSidedTopOppose{} opposed, the change is \TwoSidedMidpointMax{} points. No simulated world reproduces the observed gain one step above the midpoint (at most \TwoSidedAboveMax{} points on average, against \AboveMidpointChange{} observed).

This comparison holds the counterfactual fixed at the observed control arm and varies only the direction of reactions. It assumes that whether and how a respondent reacts is unrelated to where their answers would otherwise sit. Without that assumption the marginal distributions alone guarantee only that some respondents moved down: a sharp lower bound on the share whose mean answer fell is \MeanFallBound{}, while the corresponding bound for a rise is \MeanRiseBound{}, because a reaction that lowers only the answers of respondents just above the midpoint could refill it. An endorsement that pushed respondents toward their own policy views, without any reaction to the group, would also empty the midpoint. But it would tie answers more closely to those views and so raise the correlation between the two policies most tied to them. That correlation falls (\cref{tab:within_correlation}).

These patterns do not identify a proportion of favorable reactions. They establish that endorsements produce reactions that respondents carry across questions, that those reactions are large relative to the average effect, and that where movement in both directions is observable it occurs in both directions. The small average effect reflects opposing reactions that offset, not an absence of reactions.

\section{How Many React Favorably?}
\label{sec:model}

The within-respondent patterns show that favorable and unfavorable reactions coexist. They do not say how many respondents react favorably. Each route to that number rests on assumptions the data do not support or cannot check.

\subsection{The Measurement Model}

The measurement model of \citet{bullock2011statistical} relates ordinal policy responses to latent policy preferences and endorsement reactions. Respondent $i$ has a latent position $x_i$. For question $j$ endorsed by group $k$, the model adds a reaction $s_{ijk}$ and maps the resulting position to an observed category through question-specific loadings and cutpoints. In the baseline specification,
\begin{align}
s_{ijk} &\sim \mathcal{N}(\lambda_k,\omega_k^2), &
P(s_{ijk}>0) &= \Phi(\lambda_k/\omega_k).
\label{eq:latent_probability}
\end{align}
The mean and variance of the reaction distribution are common across policies. The reaction itself is question-specific; the specification does not assign each respondent a single fixed supporter status across all questions. The positive probability is invariant to the units of the latent scale.

A positive latent reaction need not move a response into a higher observed category. The model also includes response noise, so $P(s_{ijk}>0)$ is distinct from the probability that the observed treatment response exceeds the control potential response. Each reaction is drawn independently across a respondent's questions. That is the assumption \cref{sec:within} rejects: under it, the correlations between policy answers would not change.

I fit the baseline model without covariates using \texttt{endorse}.\footnote{The fitting script uses three chains with starting values dispersed across negative, zero, and positive mean reactions. Each chain runs for 200,000 iterations, discards 20,000, and retains every 40th draw. The largest reported Gelman--Rubin statistic across the mean and variance parameters is \MaxRhat{}, and the smallest effective sample size across mean reactions is \MinESS{}. The fits use the package's default priors.}
% MCMC settings above are fixed inputs in R/model.R, not estimated results.
\Cref{tab:endorse_results} reports posterior means and credible intervals. Averaged equally over groups, the probability of a positive latent reaction is \PrimaryShare{} (95\% credible interval \PrimaryInterval{}). This is an average of four marginal probabilities, not the probability of favoring at least one group. The Afghan Taliban have the lowest estimate, \AfghanShare{}, and a wide interval, \AfghanInterval{}. Their posterior probability of a negative mean reaction is \AfghanNegativeProbability{}; the other groups' mean-reaction intervals include zero.

\subsection{Bounds and Restrictions}

Without a model, the experiment identifies the treatment and control distributions of each answer but not how they pair within a person. Allowing every pairing of the pooled distributions bounds the fraction of person--policy responses that an endorsement raises between \LowerBound{} and \UpperBound{}. The bounds are too wide to settle the question.

Fixing the sizes of favorable and unfavorable effects in \cref{eq:ate_decomposition} turns the ATE into a share. \Cref{tab:method_comparison} collects several such restrictions. The implied shares run from \SmallSymmetricShare{} to \ExtremeShare{}, and the data contradict some of them: binary switching, for example, allows no interior responses. The response ceiling widens the range. In the control arm \CeilingShare{} of responses are at the top of the scale, where a favorable reaction cannot show. If favorable and unfavorable respondents both move one category, truncation at the top implies a favorable share of \DiscreteOneShare{} despite a negative mean.

\subsection{Direct Polling}

% Polling percentages: Pew Research Center (2012), Chapter 2 and topline, Afghan Taliban.
Pew's 2012 survey reports 14\% favorable views of the Afghan Taliban, 45\% unfavorable views, and 41\% with no opinion \citep{pew2012attitudes}. If stated opinions are classified correctly, assigning all nonrespondents to the favorable category gives an upper bound of 55\%. That bound does not allow respondents who report unfavorable opinions to conceal favorable ones.

The corresponding Afghan Taliban estimate from the 2009 endorsement data is \AfghanShare{} (95\% credible interval \AfghanInterval{}). It falls within the range obtained by varying the classification of Pew nonrespondents. But the surveys differ in year, sample composition, and measurement, and the latent probability is not the same response as direct favorability. Social desirability and fear may contribute to discrepancies; these data do not identify their contribution.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The average effect of an endorsement, the fraction of responses it raises, and the prevalence of favorable attitudes are different quantities. Random assignment identifies the first. The response distributions bound the second. The third requires a model connecting responses to attitudes, and that model can be checked against how a respondent's answers move together. The standard measurement model fails that check.

A model in which a reaction shifts a respondent's answers directly, rather than through the latent position, is the natural alternative. Latent-class versions of that model, with opponents, unaffected respondents, and supporters, did not identify the favorable share in these data: the estimate depended on how the response ceiling was modelled, the sizes of favorable and unfavorable shifts could not be separated, and simulated data with no supporters returned a positive share. The replication materials document these attempts. Population weighting and sensitivity to the assignment exclusions remain open.

Three changes to the design would identify more. Randomizing whether the group is said to support or oppose the policy separates reactions to the group from a general push away from the middle of the scale: a respondent who favors the group should move in opposite directions under the two cues, and a respondent pushed toward their own view should not. An endorsement by a neutral or widely liked actor measures how much any cue disperses answers. Policies with intermediate baseline support, or a finer response scale, leave room for favorable reactions to appear; here \CeilingShare{} of control responses are already at the top. Asking the policy questions without an endorsement in an earlier wave would go further, by observing each respondent's own shift and so the sizes of favorable and unfavorable reactions separately. Repeated-measures designs in survey experiments have generally not altered treatment effects \citep{clifford2021increasing}, although consistency pressure across waves would need to be checked.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A small negative average endorsement effect does not mean respondents are indifferent to militant groups. In these data it is the net of reactions in both directions: answers to most policy pairs become more closely related under endorsement, and the middle of the scale empties in a way that opposition alone does not produce. How many respondents react favorably is not identified. The fitted measurement model puts the probability of a positive latent reaction at \PrimaryShare{}, but under an assumption the data reject. Moving from an average response effect to a distribution of attitudes requires stating and defending the additional assumptions, and designs that flip the direction of the cue or observe each respondent's own shift would require fewer of them.
